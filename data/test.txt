Fig trees are not subjected to pruning, as such amputations are extremely harmful to them. Each cut causes a considerable loss of sap, and repeated wounds weaken the tree, compromise its vigor, and often lead to long-term decline rather than improvement.

However, fig trees are rarely given a place in gardens, chiefly because of the space they require. Each tree needs at least twenty to thirty meters to develop properly, and their extensive root systems exhaust the soil and prove highly injurious to neighboring plants.

One of the most dangerous causes of tree destruction in our orchards is the sting of the acarus. This pest attacks peach trees in particular, gradually weakening them and ultimately causing their death if effective measures are not taken in time.

Another advantage of light soil is that water passes through it much more readily than through heavy soil. In compact soils, stagnant moisture quickly causes manure to rot and plants to decay, whereas well-drained soil preserves both fertility and plant health.

We must therefore discourage those whose soil is shallow or whose ground lacks cohesion, especially when it cannot be worked easily. This defect is most often the cause of failure, as such soil cannot properly sustain cultivation over time.

Neither rain nor snow is able to penetrate these structures, which is of great importance, since excessive moisture is the most common cause of plant failure. Too much humidity proves fatal to most cultivated plants, particularly during cold seasons.

For plants sown or transplanted in March and April, which may sometimes require watering due to the fairly strong sunshine of these months, irrigation should be carried out only in the morning or at midday. Watering in the evening must be avoided, as it leaves the plants damp during the night and exposes them to damage.
