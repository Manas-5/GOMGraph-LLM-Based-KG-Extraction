WEATHER PRONOSTICS Old proverbs, the fruit of experience, should not be disdained. When swallows frequently skim the surface of land and water, when crows caw from tree tops, when lamp oil fizzles and the wick foams, it's often a sign of rain. If the stars shine brightly in the depths of a clear sky, if the fog falls and promptly settles to the ground, the weather is likely to be fine. If the sun sets behind a fiery cloud, it means wind for the night; if it hides in a blue or red mass, fear rain or wind; if it rises or sets radiant and cloudless, rejoice: it's almost certain that you'll have fine days. Yellowish-white clouds bring hail with them. Meteorology (the part of general physics dealing with atmospheric phenomena, their causes and effects) is more precise than popular tradition; it tells us, for the climate of Paris, that: 1° Rain and fine weather follow each other at short, frequent intervals in spring; in summer, the atmosphere is less changeable; showers are very common in autumn, but long-lasting rain is less frequent than in spring. 2° The wind that prevails in the first month of each equinox generally prevails for six months of the year; this is why, in some countries, it is said that, if it rains on Palm Sunday, . = 2 "1" 2 -4. . - E. 7- WEATHER PREDICTIONS. it will rain at harvest time. In Paris, if the wind begins to blow from the south, it will generally turn in the same direction around the compass rose, i.e. it will blow west, then north, then east, then back south and so on. Southwest winds are generally warm, rainy and humid; northwest winds are generally cold, bringing clouds but rarely rain. The wet autumn and temperate winter herald a cold, dry spring. If summer is rainy, winter will be harsh. If the barometer falls, you can expect rain; if it rises slowly and steadily, it generally indicates fine weather; if the mercury falls very quickly, it heralds a violent wind and squalls whose end you will learn from the rain; hence the saying: "Little rain brings big wind". Amendments. - Irrigation. Even slightly mediocre soil should always be preferred, but it should be deep and therefore easily amenable to gardening improvements, rather than soil that is better on the surface, but shallow. If you have a choice between several sloping plots, take the one facing east or south, even if it's inferior in quality to the one facing north or west; first make sure you'll be able to get water by digging wells of ordinary depth. If the soil is calcareous, it will yield in the wild: several species of Speedwell, Gallium, Sainfoin, Barberry, Viorne, Clematis, Pulsatilla Anemone; - if it is clayey, it will yield: Rush, Argentine, Coltsfoot, Saponaria; - if it is siliceous: Arearia, Viper's Bugloss, Spergula, etc. Strong or clayey earth is always soft to the touch; fired, it produces a tight, consistent brick. Iron oxide gives the soil a yellow-red color; if there are too many metal oxides, vegetation will be thin. Taste indicates the presence of salt. If the soil contains a lot of sulfur, it will release its vapors when burned on a red iron. Fire can also be used to determine the quantity of decomposing plant or animal substances contained in the earth, which essentially constitute its richness. Does a lump of this soil, when subjected to red heat, give off a gravelly smell as it burns? You can conclude from this that it contained a lot of animal matter. Fertilizers are divided into plant fertilizers and animal fertilizers. To make the former, fresh plants of all kinds must be collected and buried before the open air has robbed them of their natural moisture. Of all the fertilizers based on animal substances, the strongest and most energetic is animal black, which is wrongly thought to be good only for large-scale cultivation; use it with care in cold, clayey soils, and it will give great vigor to crucifers and root vegetables without imparting a bad taste. Stable manure can replace all other manures; it is very good for building layers; it will ferment more or less depending on whether you keep it wet or dry. Less esteemed than the previous one, as it is less active, stable manure is suitable for gypsum, sandy and calcareous soils, which are too light and very easy to heat up. Sheepfold manure mixed with stable manure is very hot. Pigeon droppings, pig manure and liquid fertilizers should also be used, although the latter should only be used to hasten the decomposition of plant debris because of their stench. The best potting soil is obtained by fermenting horned beast excrement under the soil for a whole year; it will be black, even in all its parts, soft to the touch and odorless. Common loam is the product of the complete decomposition of manure. There is also simple vegetable loam, usually made from the leaves used to build the layers. Compost is the mixture of various soils and fertilizers in proportions most suited to the cultivation of a given species of plant. For example, orange-tree soil is free-range soil with the addition of a quarter or a third of leaf compost and fatty manure or good heather soil. Dummy heather soil is made up of a mixture of half silica sand and vegetable loam, or three parts black loam from decomposed gorse and one part pure sand. Manure water, or bouillon, is obtained as follows. In a pit a few meters wide, Om.80 to one meter deep, walled in at the sides and perforated at the bottom, pour rainwater through a bung onto a compact mass of cow and horse manure from the stable; after a fortnight, drain off the liquid part. This broth restores vigor and beauty to ailing plants, and greenness to orange trees; use sparingly. Mineral substances that do not nourish plants, but modify the soil to advantage, are called soil improvers: sand, lime, marl, plaster, added earth; add ashes and soot. For gardening, marl should be mixed with equal parts of good soil; it's a good idea to spread a little every four years in the vegetable garden, if the soil is not too chalky or too light. In the coldest soil, no more than 25 to 30 liters of lime per acre should be used for initial liming, and as a maintenance dose, no more than 12 to 15 liters for the same surface area. Soot destroys insects; it should be diluted in water and sprinkled with great care. Ashes and charrée (ashes that have already been used to pour lye) are suitable for plants that don't like greasy manures; deposit ashes in small quantities at the same time as the grains. Trrigation. - Rainwater, especially when it has been laden with a greater or lesser quantity of organic matter as it flows over the soil, is rightly considered the best water for irrigation. Natural irrigation consists in dividing the vegetable garden into narrow planks, with the soil raised on both edges and a little dug in the middle, forming a gutter to receive the water. Hand-watering tools include: ordinary watering cans, the flat-sided watering can, the English watering can; continuous-spray syringes and the ordinary hand pump, the Dietz pump, and so on. Plants with soft or hairy leaves, strawberries, lettuces, melons, etc., like to be watered in the rain; in general, plants need more water in the first period of their vegetation than when the seeds and fruit begin to form; you should water more frequently plants grown for their stems and leaves (vegetables, forage), than those grown for their flowers or fruit. In summer, water your plants in the morning and evening; in spring and autumn, in the morning only; if you water in full sun, make sure your water temperature is as high as that of the plants. Well water, which is generally very bad for watering, loses some of its crudeness if it is agitated a lot and for a long time in the air before use. Spring water is often too cold and needs to be exposed to the air before use; stagnant water too often breeds moss and turns lawns yellow. The best running waters are river waters. Gardening tools and utensils. - Shelters for conserving plants: Frames, Layers, Greenhouses, Orangeries, Planters, Apartment greenhouses, etc. The spade is the gardener's main, indispensable tool. Its weight and handle length must be appropriate for the person using it. It must also be proportionate to the thickness of the plant layer. For good ordinary soil, neither too strong nor too light, we recommend the common flat-iron spade, slightly narrower at the bottom edge than at the top. For hard, stony subsoil, we recommend the curved-edge spade; instead of a straight edge, the cutting edge describes a curve with two points at the ends. The Flemish spade, slightly curved towards the middle of its length, holds back light soil that would otherwise fall back crumbled and badly turned. The pickaxe is used to make holes, to uproot trees, and to break up compacted soil that is difficult to break up with the spade. If this is not enough, a pick is used, which differs from the pickaxe in the strength and length of its thick, narrow blade, and in its very penetrating point. The hoe works more superficially, but also more quickly than the spade; it is used for ploughing at the foot of trees or in nurseries, for mounding potatoes, etc. The ordinary hoe has a carved blade. The ordinary hoe has a square blade, slightly curved on the inside, with a short handle, which forces the worker to work bent over, moving straight ahead; when working with a spade, he walks backwards. To break up hard, dry soil, we use a hoe with a 41 Fig. 1 - Hoe. triangular blade. Potatoes and other root vegetables are dug up using a hoe with two flat teeth of varying length. After the spade, the hoe is the most widely used gardening tool. Its blade, much lighter and narrower than that of the hoe, is used to make holes for sowing beans, buttering certain plants, planting potatoes and so on. The second method is called hoeing. The serfouette differs from the hoe in that it has a narrower blade; the forked serfouette has two tines forming a fork on the opposite side to the blade; it is used to bury soil around small flowerbeds, and to pull up bindweed, quackgrass roots, etc., etc. The disbudder, either hand-held or attached to a stick of varying length, is used to cut buds and twigs from tree trunks; the Fig. 2. - Disbudders. three forms indicated above are the most advantageous. The weeder, whose use is indicated by its very name, is used to break the hardened crusts that form around plants during drought, and to remove weeds. The blunter is a kind of scraper used to clear the trunks and branches of fruit trees of parasitic plants such as mosses and lichens. The rake is also used to clear paths of weeds, by cutting the roots below the collar. The tracing tool is a wooden instrument used to mark out radii on the ground for planting and sowing. Imagine a kind of compass with a stationary branch in the middle, and two outer branches that can be moved on hinges, but whose spacing can be controlled at will by means of pegs and corresponding holes drilled in a curved piece of wood. The planter is made from a piece of hard wood with one end rounded and slightly pointed; the other end, curved and forming a more or less obtuse angle, provides a convenient grip for the hand. The simplest is a small, trowel-shaped spade, which is used to gently remove the plant, which is then placed in the hole prepared for it; other transplanters have blades bent into half and three-quarter circles, and others form a complete cylinder. Wooden shovels must have a strip of sheet metal lining the bottom of the flat part; iron shovels with curved, slightly concave blades are also used. Scissors of different sizes should be made of good steel, with a stag-horn handle that retains its roughness and is blackened, and with a wide, protruding edge, so that the instrument remains firm in the hand; Rouen scissors have a well-deserved reputation. Among the main grafting tools we'll mention the ordinary grafting tool, whose handle is fitted at its base with a spatula-shaped piece of ivory used to lift the bark; the blade folds back like that of a knife. The grafting tool has a retracting blade or a sliding blade with a spatula; the metro-grafting tool uses a pressure screw to measure the depth of the graft; the grafting tool is used for slit grafting, the hazelnut grafting tool is used for Pontoise grafting; the grafting tool-shears is used for ring grafting. Pruning shears are available in a range of sizes; when using this instrument, care must be taken to turn the sharp blade away from, and not towards, the branch to be cut; the pruning shear is preferred for large branches. The sickle should have a sharp cutting edge and a very thick back. Saws should always be able to work between the branches of bushy trees; the rough work done by the saw is completed with the pruning shears. Crescents, loppers and pruners are used for pruning and trimming large trees; they must be attached Fig. 4. - Loppers. firmly to their handles, which are several meters long. The pruning shear is a scissor-shaped instrument fitted at the end of a long handle to reach the highest branches easily; it is set in motion by pulling one of the blades with a string; a sort of net, attached below, catches the cut part. Towards the end of winter, before the eggs have hatched, the weeding must be done. Shears are large, strong scissors, armed with two sturdy handles, used for pruning young shrubs, palisades of greenery, hedges and so on. The cast-iron, wooden or stone roller consists of a hollow or solid cylinder, fitted with a handle, which the gardener pushes in front of him; the hollow cylinder is made more or less heavy by loading its inner part to a greater or lesser extent. The trowel, fairly well defined by its name, is used to place the soil in the pots, and to open the holes where the pots will be placed. The cueille-fruit or cueilloir, carried on a long handle, is used to pick, without damaging them, fruit placed beyond the reach of the arm. Shelters are used to protect plants from the cold, strong winds and excessive sunlight. Let's list the main types of shelter. An ados is an elevation of earth in the shape of the back of a hut, wider at the bottom than at the top, generally leaning against a wall at the top, facing south, with a slope of Om, 32 or so; ados provides early crops, and makes them grow faster than on horizontal ground: lettuces, Raves, four-season French peas, etc. Stone walls are the best of all shelters for fruit trees; white walls reflect the sun's heat, black walls absorb it; on the south side, they hasten the ripening of fruit and vegetables and protect frigid trees from the cold; on the north side, they defend plants in cold countries from too much sun. In the absence of stones, plank walls of roughly the same height (2.50 m) are built and, to make them durable, coated with tar. Two rows of Jerusalem artichokes, with their rapid growth and abundant foliage, make excellent windbreaks for cold and temperate greenhouse plants that spend the summer outdoors; for seedlings that fear drought, and so on. Movable canopies, made of planks or straw, placed over espaliers, protect them from late spring frosts; for the same purpose, mulch or canvas is spread in front of flowering trees; greenhouses and frames are covered with it to break the sun's rays in summer and to cushion the impact of hailstones. Mulch is a layer of short litter or half-consumed manure, used from March onwards to retain water from rainfall and watering; to smother the seeds of certain weeds; to prevent the soil from cracking, drying out and hardening; and to protect delicate plants from spring frosts. Glass cloches are laid flat on the ground when the plants they are designed to shelter can do without air. If the heat is too great, or if the nights are cold, these cloches are covered with a bit of long straw or heavy canvas; if the temperature is mild, the cloches can be raised a little on the south side. Verrines are glass compartments joined together by lead strips; they have one or two small windows on their facets, which can be opened at will to give air to the plants thus enclosed; verrines are very useful, but expensive. Cages are closed or open cylinders made of wicker, with pointed feet that are sunk into the ground. This type of shelter provides certain plants with the amount of light they can withstand during their early development, and protects them from animals. Counter-sunshades (against the sun) are halves of flower pots, cut lengthwise, which are placed in front of a young plant to shelter it from heat and wind; they are mainly used for cucumbers, gherkins, pumpkins and other cucurbits. The box is usually made of wood and varies in length from 1.30 to 4 meters, with a width of only one meter when it is to contain a warm layer; you can make it 1.30 meters wide for warm or dull layers. The back of the box is always higher than the front, to give the panel a greater or lesser slope. Air is supplied from behind or in front by lifting the panels with a rack, sliding them gently backwards, forwards or sideways. Overlapping panes of glass must be both very transparent and fairly thick. The box rests on the ground, and even sinks a few centimetres into it, when the artificial heat is produced by fermenting manure; if a thermosiphon is used, the box rests on a 2 wooden floor, under which the pipes of the device pass; an outer covering of hot manure hastens or forces the plants contained in the box; a simple bedding of dry leaves protects them from the cold. Each mobile frame is fitted with two iron handles. The fixed frame, 2.10 to 2.80 m high at the back and Om.70 at the front, is a kind of small greenhouse, in which we place, planted in pots, full-ground shrubs, simply to preserve them from the cold, or to force them: in the first case, an outer cover of dry leaves or slats is sufficient; in the second, a manure stove renewed every 15 or 20 days is needed; a deaf layer is established inside, where the pipes of a thermosiphon are laid under the boards supporting the soil. For the construction of mobile or fixed frames, oak wood is rightly preferred, tarred on the inside and oil-painted on the outside. On economical white wooden frames, the glass panes are replaced by linseed oil-coated canvas or paper. The layers are made of manure, either on its own or mixed with dead leaves, moss, etc., which can heat up and carry the heat it gives off for varying lengths of time. The layers do not feed the plants directly, but must be covered with a more or less thick bed of potting soil or garden soil, combined in various proportions. They need to be protected by natural or artificial shelter from northerly winds, and placed on dry rather than damp soil. For warm layers, we use horse manure in all its fermentation stage, fresh from the stable, well mixed and well watered. This manure has the advantage over all others of interrupting fermentation when dry, and resuming it as soon as it is watered. Recent manure stoves or belts maintain or restore heat to these layers, whose length is indeterminate, whose width varies from Om, 80 to 1m, 30, and whose height from Om, 80 to one meter. The same dimensions, or more or less, are observed for warm layers built with a mixture of horse manure, cow manure and leaves; for deaf layers built, if you like, with the same materials as the previous ones, and having the distinctive character of being established in trenches of Om, 30, and of being domed on top. You will notice, moreover, that the muted layers retain their heat for a long time, but cannot be renewed with the help of stoves; it is nevertheless a good idea to line the paths separating these layers with a thick layer of dry, hot manure. As a general rule, to build up a layer properly, you need to mix the manures well, spread them out in equal bunches, tamp them down, strike the entire surface with the back of the fork, and clearly determine the vertical edges. Some gardeners build up their layers in large manure beds superimposed lengthwise and widthwise; others prefer, with good reason, to build up one end of the layer immediately to the desired height, and continue in this way to the other end, working backwards. It's better to wet manure before placement than afterwards. In tarpaulins and hot greenhouses, layers are made with tannin. Mushroom beds, finished in the shape of a roof, are made in dark, draught-free cellars or storerooms, with donkey, horse or mule dung well soaked in the urine of these animals; these beds produce on their own; otherwise, they must be lined with fragments of mushroom spawn arranged in a chessboard at Om, 16 from each other and flush with the surface, or with very small pieces of young fresh mushrooms picked and laid in fresh water. They are usually built of masonry, with the floor lower by Om, 50 to Om,60 than the outside floor; they are high enough inside for a man of average height to be able to stand on them everywhere; they are heated by means of furnaces, the openings of which are arranged in such a way that the smoke cannot harm the pineapples, Cape bulbous plants, vines, heathers, etc., which they usually contain. The glazing, hinged to the back wall, lifts from the front throughout the summer, night and day, except in rainy weather; tarpaulins and mats are used to protect against excessive heat and frost; a thick layer of river sand or even clinker protects the bottom from insects. The orangery differs from the greenhouse proper in that it has no glazed roof, is built entirely of masonry, and has large windows on the south side; the plants receive light from only one side, so this type of building should not be too deep; the temperature in the orangery must never fall below zero in winter, and to obtain this result, a permanent heating appliance is not necessary, so a stove is all that's needed. The cold greenhouse, with one or two slopes and a flat or domed surface, is used to house plants that naturally suspend their growth in winter without losing their leaves, or that continue to live as they would in their native land where winter is unknown: Camellias, Pelargoniums, Calceolaria, Indian Azaleas, Magnolias, etc. As in the orangery, it's enough that it doesn't freeze in the cold greenhouse; the Dutch greenhouse and the Flemish greenhouse differ little from the ordinary cold greenhouse: covers and straw mats protect against frost and too much solar radiation. The winter garden, which has become very fashionable in recent years, is a two-sided greenhouse with walkways and a pond. The temperate greenhouse is the most useful of all greenhouses; if it weren't so expensive to build, it would have long since replaced all the orangeries built at such great expense over the past two centuries. It is made with one or two sides, and is preferably heated by a thermosiphon. This device consists of a hermetically sealed boiler, under which fire is lit at will; cast-iron or copper pipes start from the boiler and, after describing more or less long convolutions in the greenhouse, return to the boiler at a point on its lateral surface lower than their starting point; in this way, ascending currents of hot water and descending currents of cold water are established until the temperature of the entire contents of the boiler is perfectly balanced.
